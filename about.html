My Story

Background

Who I Am

The Work

I work across the full analytics pipeline: data cleaning, feature engineering, model training, evaluation, and building dashboards that hold up after handoff. Most of my projects involve messy or imbalanced data where the preprocessing decisions end up mattering more than the model choice. Switching from a Random Forest to XGBoost rarely moves the needle as much as fixing how the training labels were constructed.

The Approach

I spend more time on problem framing and feature design than most people expect. A model trained on leaky features or a poorly structured target variable will look great in training and fail in practice. I've caught that mistake more than once, which is why I check for it deliberately now. It's not a glamorous part of the process, but it's usually where the real problems are hiding.

The Direction

My background is in Statistics and Programming, and my project work has moved progressively toward ML applications in operational contexts: fraud, credit risk, logistics, forecasting. I'm looking for roles where the data is taken seriously enough that people notice when the analysis is wrong, not just when it's missing.

The gap between a model that works on paper and one that's actually useful in practice is mostly a data quality and framing problem. I've spent more time on that gap than on tuning hyperparameters, and I think that's probably the right allocation. Timothy Maina

Focus Areas

What I Work On

Problem types I've worked through in detail, not just read about. Each one has gone through the full pipeline at least once.

Predictive Modelling

Classification and regression on operational datasets. I've worked on fraud detection, churn prediction, credit risk, and logistics delay modelling. The common thread across all of them is handling class imbalance, catching feature leakage early, and making threshold decisions that reflect what the wrong prediction actually costs in practice rather than just what maximises a headline metric.

Business Intelligence and Dashboards

Building Power BI and Tableau dashboards that people actually open after the first week. I pay attention to filter logic, layout, and what happens when the underlying data changes or someone who wasn't in the original meeting tries to use it. In practice most dashboards go through at least two or three revision cycles before the layout settles into something genuinely navigable.

Forecasting and Time Series

Demand forecasting with ARIMA and Prophet on multi-SKU inventory data, and sales forecasting layered into executive dashboards. The practical challenge is usually that no single model works well across all product types. Prophet handled seasonality better for most SKUs in my inventory project, but ARIMA held up more reliably on flat demand profiles. Running both and comparing is slower, but it produces more defensible output.

Segmentation and Clustering

Customer segmentation using K-Means on RFM features, and multi-channel marketing attribution using Markov Chain models. K-Means requires committing to a cluster count before you know what the clusters will look like, which takes a few iterations. The Markov Chain work was more involved, mostly in validating that the transition sequences were constructed correctly before running the removal effect calculations.

Operational and Exploratory Analytics

Profiling datasets, identifying anomalies, building reporting pipelines that produce consistent output. A lot of this is unglamorous but necessary: cleaning inconsistent categoricals, reshaping wide tables, tracking down where nulls are actually coming from, and writing SQL that someone else can modify six months later without breaking everything downstream.

Feature Engineering and Pipeline Design

Building features from raw transactional or operational data, handling missing values, encoding categoricals, and constructing pipelines that produce the same output when run again on new data. In my logistics project the feature engineering took longer than the modelling, partly because several early feature candidates turned out to be proxies for the target variable and had to be rebuilt from scratch. That kind of iteration is normal, but it's not always accounted for in how long a project takes.

Working Style

How I Approach Projects

01

Understand the data before modelling anything

I profile the dataset first: distributions, missing values, duplicates, outliers, class balance. This usually surfaces problems that would cause the model to fail silently if ignored. It also tends to reveal which features are actually informative before any modelling happens, which saves time later. Skipping this step to get to the model faster tends to create more work, not less.

02

Check for leakage before tuning anything

A model with suspiciously high validation scores is usually a leakage problem, not a good model. I check this explicitly, especially when target construction involves derived variables. In my logistics project I had R² of 0.99 before finding and removing the leaky features that caused it.

03

Choose evaluation metrics based on what errors cost

Accuracy is often the wrong metric. For churn I optimise for recall because a missed at-risk customer is more costly than sending a retention offer to someone who wasn't going to churn anyway. For fraud the concern is different: too many false positives and analysts stop trusting the model entirely, which makes it useless regardless of its technical performance. The metric choice should come from the actual consequences of each error type, not from what's easiest to report.

04

Build outputs that stay useful after the project ends

A dashboard that requires the original analyst to explain it is only half-finished. I try to structure reports so that someone unfamiliar with the project can navigate them without a walkthrough. This usually means fewer views, clearer labels, and some annotation of what each number means in context. It also means being willing to simplify things that look impressive but don't actually help someone make a decision.
