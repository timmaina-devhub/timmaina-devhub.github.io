Customer Churn Analysis & Segmentation

The starting point was a simple question: which customers are about to leave, and how do you tell them apart from customers who just buy infrequently? This one ended up being one of my favourite projects — the feature engineering clicked, the clusters actually meant something, and recall tuning felt like a real decision instead of an academic exercise.

The Problem

Retention efforts were being applied uniformly across all customers. There was no way to tell who was genuinely at risk versus who just hadn't bought recently. Loyal and one-time buyers looked the same without some structure around behaviour.

My Approach

I engineered recency, frequency, and monetary features from raw order data, then trained a logistic regression model tuned for recall rather than accuracy, since missing an at-risk customer is the more costly error. K-Means segmentation ran separately on the RFM features to group customers into behaviorally distinct clusters. The whole thing feeds into a Tableau dashboard.

Key Outcomes

  • Days since last purchase and refund rate came out as the strongest churn signals, ahead of order count
  • K-Means produced 3 stable segments with meaningfully different purchase and refund patterns
  • Recall-optimised threshold reduced missed at-risk customers at the cost of some precision, which was the right tradeoff here
  • Dashboard filters by segment so retention actions can be targeted rather than broadcast

Fraud Detection Command Centre

The dataset was severely imbalanced, rule-based detection was producing too many false positives, and the fraud patterns were shifting over time. I built a behavioural feature pipeline from scratch and compared three models before settling on Random Forest, then connected it to a Tableau dashboard for ongoing monitoring.

The Problem

The existing rule-based system flagged too many legitimate transactions and missed fraud that didn't fit the predefined patterns. The goal was a model that could catch evolving behaviour without flooding analysts with false alerts.

My Approach

I built features around transaction velocity within time windows, device and IP repetition across accounts, and hour-of-day patterns. Applied SMOTE to handle class imbalance, then tested Logistic Regression, XGBoost, and Random Forest. Random Forest had the best precision-recall balance at a threshold that kept false positives manageable. Output goes into Tableau for analyst review.

Key Outcomes

  • Transaction velocity in the last hour was the strongest single predictor, well ahead of amount
  • Device and IP repetition caught a distinct pattern of repeat offenders the rule system was missing
  • SMOTE helped with minority class recall, but threshold tuning did most of the precision-recall work
  • Tableau dashboard tracks flagged entities, fraud rate trends, and estimated financial exposure by period

Multi-Channel Marketing Attribution & ROI Optimization

Most marketing attribution in practice defaults to last-click, which gives all the credit to whichever channel happened to be last in the sequence. I wanted to see how a probabilistic model would distribute credit differently, and whether that changes which channels look worth spending on.

The Problem

Last-click attribution consistently overstates the value of bottom-funnel channels and understates anything that appears earlier in a customer journey. Budget decisions made on that data tend to cut channels that were actually doing useful work upstream.

My Approach

Built a Markov Chain model over customer journey sequences to estimate each channel's removal effect, i.e., how much conversion probability drops when that channel is taken out. Separately, trained a Random Forest on campaign-level features to predict revenue, using time-based train/test splits to avoid leakage from future data. The two outputs together give both attribution credit and a revenue forecast.

Key Outcomes

  • Markov attribution redistributed credit away from last-click channels, some mid-funnel channels looked considerably more valuable under this model
  • Revenue prediction hit ~0.79 R² on hold-out data using time-aware validation
  • CPA was the dominant revenue predictor, more so than impressions or raw spend
  • Efficiency metrics (CPA, conversion rate) consistently outperformed volume metrics in feature importance

Demand Forecasting & Inventory Optimization System

Stockouts and excess inventory are both expensive, but they're caused by different forecasting failures. This project compared ARIMA and Prophet on multi-SKU demand data, then built out Safety Stock, ROP, and EOQ calculations on top of whichever forecast was more accurate per product.

The Problem

About 12% of SKU-days had stockouts. At the same time, a lot of capital was sitting in slow-moving lines. The existing Excel forecasting didn't account for seasonality, and supplier lead time variability made standard safety stock calculations unreliable.

My Approach

Ran ARIMA and Prophet on each SKU's demand history and compared forecast error. Prophet handled seasonality better for most SKUs. On top of the forecast output I calculated safety stock using historical demand variance and lead time distributions, then derived reorder points and EOQ per SKU. Everything feeds into a Power BI dashboard showing stockout risk and reorder recommendations.

Key Outcomes

  • Prophet outperformed ARIMA on seasonal SKUs; ARIMA held up better on flat demand profiles
  • 1,900+ stockout events identified across the period, concentrated in a subset of high-velocity SKUs
  • Supplier delay data showed a meaningful portion of shipments arriving outside stated lead time, which affected safety stock sizing
  • ROP and EOQ calculations gave planners a starting point that accounts for demand variability, not just average demand

Customer Credit Risk Assessment Model

I picked this up partly because I wanted to work on something where a wrong prediction has real financial consequences. It felt different from the earlier projects — the cost of a missed signal isn't just an annoyed user, it's money. I spent more time reviewing the class balance and decision thresholds here than on model selection.

The Problem

Default rate was running at 18.3% against an industry benchmark of around 9.5%. A single approval threshold applied to all loan sizes regardless of borrower profile. About 35% of applicants had informal income, making traditional credit assessment unreliable. A subset of rejected applicants were reapplying repeatedly to bypass the system.

My Approach

Rebuilt the target variable using a composite risk score from borrower financial behaviour and credit characteristics. Added controlled noise within credit score bands to reduce target leakage. Engineered loan-to-income and debt-to-income ratios, aggregated account-level behavioural patterns, applied SMOTE, then compared Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost on precision-recall curves rather than just accuracy.

Key Outcomes

  • XGBoost reached 0.76 F1-score with ~77% recall and ~75% precision on the reconstructed target
  • The stochastic noise added to credit score bands meaningfully reduced leakage from the target construction step
  • Debt-to-income ratio and loan-to-income ratio were more informative than raw credit score in most model configurations
  • Precision-recall tradeoffs were evaluated explicitly given the cost asymmetry between false approvals and false rejections

Delivery Downtime Prediction & Route Optimization

The tricky part of this project wasn't the modelling, it was building features that actually represented route difficulty without using variables that would leak information from the target. Traffic and weather aren't directly in most logistics datasets, so I had to proxy them from what was available.

The Problem

Delivery SLA breaches were happening regularly and there was no good way to predict which shipments were most likely to be delayed before they went out. The contributing factors, driver behaviour, route difficulty, time of day, conditions, interact in non-obvious ways that a simple rule can't capture.

My Approach

I built proxy features for congestion from GPS speed deviation patterns, and weather risk from historical delay rates by region and season. Removed several features that initially looked useful but were essentially restatements of the delay target. Trained Random Forest and XGBoost regressors; XGBoost generalised better on the holdout set. The model predicts delay hours, not just a binary flag.

Key Outcomes

  • R² of ~0.70 after removing leaky features that had artificially inflated it to ~0.99
  • Driver performance metrics and route-level historical delay rate were the most predictive inputs
  • Traffic and weather proxied from internal data without external API calls, which made the pipeline more portable
  • The feature engineering process took longer than the modelling itself, which is probably realistic for this type of problem

Superstore Sales Executive Dashboard

This started as a straightforward dashboard project on the Superstore dataset and turned into a proper forecasting exercise when it became clear that the sales trends had enough structure to model. ARIMA and Prophet both ran on the time series; results are embedded directly in the dashboard.

The Problem

Leadership had no single view of which regions, product categories, or customer segments were profitable versus which were quietly losing money. Pulling that together required combining four years of order, returns, and segment data that wasn't structured for analysis.

My Approach

Cleaned and structured the data in Excel before connecting it to Tableau. Built KPI views for sales, profit margin, and order volume with regional and category drill-downs. Ran ARIMA and Prophet on the monthly sales series to generate a 12-month forward forecast, then added that as a separate view in the dashboard so the history and projection sit in the same place.

Key Outcomes

  • Several sub-categories were generating positive revenue but negative profit, which wasn't visible in the existing reports
  • Regional performance varied significantly when broken down by segment, not just geography
  • 12-month forecast gives planners a baseline to work from, with visible uncertainty bands
  • The full analysis fits on one dashboard screen, which makes it usable rather than just comprehensive
